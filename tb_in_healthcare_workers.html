TUBERCULOSIS IN HEALTH CARE WORKERS

Respiratory Medicine  |  NTEP-Based Notes
10 MARKS

1. INTRODUCTION

  • TB is the leading occupational infectious disease among Health Care Workers (HCWs) globally.
  • The contagious nature of TB was recognized long before its bacteriological aetiology — the air-borne route makes HCWs uniquely vulnerable.
  • TB among HCWs is now considered an occupational disease (Kilinc et al., 2002).
  • India contributes ~27% of the global TB burden (NTEP India TB Report 2023), making occupational TB a priority concern.
  • Median prevalence of Latent TB Infection (LTBI) in HCWs: 63% in Low-Middle Income Countries (LMICs); ~47% in high TB-burden countries.
  • Annual Risk of Infection (ARI) in HCWs ranged from 0.09% to 10% — up to 100× higher than the general population in Western Europe.
Key Fact (India): A study from Chandigarh showed an overall TB incidence of 17.3 per 1,000 person-years among resident doctors, with a risk of 11.2 cases/1,000 person-years of exposure.

3. RECOGNITION OF TB TRANSMISSION IN HCWs

A. Tuberculin Skin Test (TST)

  • Most frequently used marker of TB infection in HCWs.
  • Interpretation is confounded by prior BCG vaccination and environmental mycobacterial exposure.
  • Cut-offs (as per Indian studies):
    • ≥10 mm — supports diagnosis in patients with strong clinical suspicion of TB.
    • ≥15 mm — more suitable in other patients (Chandigarh study).
  • TST conversion (negative → positive) is considered evidence of new occupational infection.

B. Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRAs)

  • Used extensively for LTBI diagnosis in HCWs in both low and high TB prevalence settings.
  • No distinct advantage of IGRAs over TST for contact screening in high TB-burden settings.
  • Due to higher cost and requirement of blood sample, TST remains the preferred method in high TB burden settings (Indian NTEP approach).
  • Molecular techniques (DNA fingerprinting, RFLP) can confirm occupational transmission from a specific source.

5. CLINICAL FEATURES & DIAGNOSIS IN HCWs

  • Clinical presentation of TB in HCWs does not differ from that in the general population.
  • Pulmonary TB is the most common form; extra-pulmonary TB also reported (0.34–1.57/1,000 EPTB cases — Vellore, 10-year review).
  • HCWs tend to self-treat and delay seeking formal medical advice — increases risk of transmission in hospitals.
  • NTEP recommends active, symptom-based case-finding for all HCWs via a standardized symptoms questionnaire.
NTEP Definition of TB Suspect in HCWs: Any HCW with respiratory or constitutional symptoms of even 1 week's duration (not explained by an alternative aetiology) should be considered a TB suspect and investigated — this is a lower threshold than the standard RNTCP/NTEP definition for the general population.

7. RISK ASSESSMENT

  • Risk of transmission is proportional to contaminated air inhaled — demonstrated in naval vessel TB outbreak.
  • Mathematical models (Wells-Riley equation) quantitatively link room ventilation to infection probability.
  • Key predictors of TST conversion in HCWs:
    • Working in in-patient / out-patient facilities.
    • History of TB exposure in the previous year.
    • Performing aerosol-generating procedures (bronchoscopy, sputum induction).
  • NTEP mandates regular assessment of TB status among all HCWs as part of facility-level infection control.

8. CONTROLLING OCCUPATIONAL TUBERCULOSIS

Two Pillars (NTEP / National AIC Guidelines 2026):
  • (i) Early Diagnosis and Treatment
  • (ii) Prevention of Infection and Disease

A. Administrative Controls

  • Decompression of crowded areas (reduce patient load per ward).
  • Segregation / fast-tracking of patients with respiratory symptoms.
  • Minimize duration of hospitalization of infectious TB patients.
  • Mandatory Infection Control Training of all HCWs.
  • Regular TB screening of all HCWs (NTEP: annual TST or symptom questionnaire).
  • Standardized TB suspect definition with lower symptom threshold for HCWs.
  • Notification of diagnosed TB cases among HCWs under NTEP.

B. Environmental Controls

  • Adequate Ventilation:
    • Natural ventilation: minimum 6–12 air changes per hour (ACH) recommended.
    • Mechanical ventilation with negative pressure rooms for infectious patients.
    • Caution: mechanical ventilation can spread infection throughout the building if air is recirculated.
  • HEPA (High-Efficiency Particulate Air) Filters:
    • Remove ~100% of particles >0.3 µm in diameter.
    • Used in rooms, bronchoscopy suites, sputum induction booths.
    • HEPA-filtered self-contained booths available commercially.
  • Ultraviolet Germicidal Irradiation (UVGI):
    • UV directed at upper room air — kills mycobacteria.
    • Advantages: less resistance to airflow, smaller blowers.
    • Disadvantages: risk of superficial UV exposure to skin/eyes if ceiling is low.
    • No evidence of systemic immunosuppression from germicidal UV.
  • Separate cubicles/booths for cough-inducing procedures (sputum induction, bronchoscopy).
  • Biomedical waste management and appropriate disposal of excreta/linen.

C. Personal Protection Measures

  • Masks for Patients: Simple gauze/surgical masks — limit release of droplets from the patient's mouth; useful for limited durations.
  • Respirators for HCWs:
    • More effective than simple masks — filters particles via impaction, filtration, or electrostatic attraction.
    • Properly worn respirator can prevent up to 90% of exposure if fitted correctly.
    • N95 Respirator:
      • Filters ≥95% of airborne particles ≥0.3 µm.
      • Must be fit-tested for each individual HCW.
      • Mandatory for HCWs performing aerosol-generating procedures (bronchoscopy, sputum induction, intubation).
      • Recommended by NTEP for all HCWs entering isolation rooms or treating confirmed / suspected TB.
    • FFP2/FFP3 Masks: European equivalent of N95; similarly recommended in high-risk settings.
  • Regular mask fit-testing and training on correct donning/doffing is mandatory.
  • Surgical masks are NOT a substitute for N95 respirators in high-risk aerosol exposure settings.

10. LATENT TB INFECTION (LTBI) IN HCWs — NTEP APPROACH

NTEP Position on LTBI Treatment: India does not routinely recommend preventive therapy for LTBI in all HCWs with positive TST/IGRA due to the high background prevalence. However, high-risk subgroups (HIV-positive HCWs, recent converters, immunocompromised HCWs) are candidates for Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT).

A. Isoniazid Preventive Therapy (IPT) — As per NTEP

  • Indication: HIV-positive HCWs with positive TST (≥5 mm) regardless of prior BCG; recent TST converters who are immunocompromised.
  • Regimen: Isoniazid (INH) 5 mg/kg/day (max 300 mg/day) for 6 months.
  • Active TB must be excluded before starting IPT.
  • Efficacy: Reduces risk of progression from LTBI to active TB by 60–90% in compliant patients.
  • Shorter regimen: 3HP (Isoniazid + Rifapentine weekly × 12 weeks) — not yet routinely used under NTEP but under evaluation.
  • Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) supplementation co-administered to prevent INH-induced peripheral neuropathy.

B. BCG Vaccination in HCWs

  • Childhood BCG vaccination is universal in India and provides protection mainly against severe forms in children.
  • Adult BCG revaccination of HCWs is NOT routinely recommended under the NTEP (insufficient evidence of benefit in adults).
  • Novel TB vaccines under trials (e.g., M72/AS01E) — future role in HCW protection.

Work Isolation and Return-to-Work Policy (NTEP)

  • HCW with smear-positive pulmonary TB: should be relieved of patient care duties until declared non-infectious.
  • Non-infectious criteria (NTEP-aligned):
    • At least 2 weeks of effective anti-TB treatment completed.
    • Two consecutive sputum smear-negative results on separate days.
    • Clinical improvement (apyrexia, reduced cough).
  • HCW with smear-negative / extra-pulmonary TB: can continue patient care with appropriate precautions.
  • MDR-TB or XDR-TB in HCW: prolonged work isolation until culture-negative confirmed — strict policy required.
  • Confidentiality of the HCW's TB diagnosis must be protected; stigma management is essential.
  • Occupational records and compensation benefits must be documented.

13. DRUG-RESISTANT TB IN HCWs — A SPECIAL CONCERN

  • Healthcare settings are amplifiers of MDR-TB transmission — multiple large nosocomial outbreaks documented globally.
  • A key driver: inadequate infection control + poor ventilation in crowded facilities.
  • Room size is a critical factor in MDR-TB nosocomial spread (Escombe et al.).
  • NTEP mandates universal DST (Drug Sensitivity Testing) for all TB patients:
    • CBNAAT (Xpert MTB/RIF) at diagnosis for rapid RIF resistance detection.
    • Line Probe Assay (LPA) for INH and fluoroquinolone resistance.
    • Liquid culture + DST for second-line drugs if MDR confirmed.
  • HCWs with MDR/XDR-TB:
    • Mandatory prolonged work absence until culture-negative.
    • Registered under NTEP DR-TB programme; managed at designated DR-TB centres.
    • Contact tracing extends to all patients and staff in the unit.
  • Exposure to MDR-TB patients without adequate PPE is a notifiable occupational event.

15. CHALLENGES IN MANAGING TB AMONG HCWs IN INDIA

  • Stigma and self-medication: HCWs often avoid disclosing symptoms, leading to delayed diagnosis and continued transmission.
  • Low index of suspicion: Familiarity with TB leads to attitudinal desensitization ("it won't happen to me").
  • Inadequate infrastructure: Most public hospitals in India lack negative-pressure isolation rooms and air-quality monitoring equipment.
  • Non-compliance with PPE: Inconsistent N95 usage, especially during routine care of undiagnosed smear-positive patients.
  • Absent institutional TB policies: Many hospitals — including tertiary ones — lack formal HCW TB surveillance programmes or Infection Control Plans (ICPs).
  • Limited LTBI management: No systematic programme for identifying and treating LTBI in HCWs in most Indian institutions.
  • Rising DR-TB burden: MDR/XDR-TB in healthcare settings is increasingly reported; standard infection control measures inadequate without universal DST.
  • Under-reporting: TB among HCWs is significantly under-notified; few hospitals maintain occupational TB registers as mandated by NTEP.

17. CONCLUSION

  • TB in HCWs is a preventable occupational hazard that demands systematic institutional and policy-level action.
  • The three-level control hierarchy — administrative → environmental → personal protective equipment — remains the cornerstone of prevention under NTEP.
  • Early case-finding with a lower symptom threshold, universal DST, and prompt treatment under DOT are critical to protect HCWs and prevent nosocomial spread.
  • India's NTEP has provided a robust framework; the challenge lies in its uniform implementation across all levels of healthcare facilities.
  • Recognition of TB as an occupational disease entitles affected HCWs to legal protection, compensation, and stigma-free management.
  • Future priorities: universal LTBI screening, novel vaccine deployment, mandatory ICP audits, and electronic HCW TB registries integrated with NTEP's Ni-kshay portal.
Exam Key Point: In a 10-mark answer, always mention — (1) occupational risk is 2–10× higher, (2) three-tier control hierarchy, (3) N95 vs. surgical mask distinction, (4) NTEP lower symptom threshold for HCWs, (5) work-isolation criteria, and (6) rights under NTEP including Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana.
