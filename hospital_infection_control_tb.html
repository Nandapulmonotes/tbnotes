Hospital Infection Control of Tuberculosis

📋 10-Mark Answer 🏭 Pulmonary Medicine 📚 NTEP Guidelines | WHO | CDC ⏰ ~1000 words | 5–7 pages
1. Introduction
  • Tuberculosis (TB) is transmitted via airborne droplet nuclei (1–5 µm) generated by coughing, sneezing, or aerosol-generating procedures.
  • Nosocomial (health care–associated) TB transmission is a significant threat to health care workers (HCWs), patients, and visitors.
  • Prevalence of TB is inherently higher in health care facilities than in the general population, amplifying transmission risk.
  • India's National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) and its 2010 Airborne Infection Control Guidelines mandate structured infection control in all health care settings.
  • Goal: Eliminate TB by 2030 (SDG target) and reduce incidence to <1/million by 2050 — impossible without robust infection control.
📌 Key Fact (Fennelly et al.)
Cough aerosol from TB patients contains 3–4 CFU to a maximum of 633 CFU of viable, infectious Mycobacterium tuberculosis.
2. Why Hospitals Are High-Risk ("Institutional Amplifiers")
  • Unsuspected TB patients remain undiagnosed for weeks, visiting multiple health facilities while infectious.
  • Crowded OPDs, poorly ventilated wards, shared waiting areas — all favour droplet nuclei accumulation.
  • Delayed diagnosis → prolonged infectious period → more exposure.
  • Immunocompromised patients (HIV, transplant, malignancy) at extreme risk once exposed.
  • Aerosol-generating procedures (bronchoscopy, sputum induction, intubation) enormously amplify risk.
  • MDR-TB and XDR-TB outbreaks in hospitals have been associated with high death rates, especially in HIV-positive patients.
⚠ Risk Factor: Air Change Rate
Medical students training in rooms with 16.2 m³/bed had a 25.5% TST conversion rate vs. 12.7% in hospitals with 41.4 m³/bed — highlighting ventilation as a critical protective factor.

4. Tier 1 — Administrative Controls

Administrative controls are the most feasible and cost-effective measures, especially in resource-limited settings (NTEP 2010, CDC).

4a. Key Administrative Measures
  • Rapid identification of patients with respiratory symptoms (triage).
  • Separation / isolation of suspected or confirmed TB patients.
  • Applying surgical masks on coughing TB patients not yet segregated.
  • Fast-tracking through facility to reduce contact time with others.
  • Rapid diagnosis and prompt initiation of anti-TB treatment (DOTS / NTEP regimen).
  • Minimise hospitalisation — ambulatory treatment wherever feasible.
  • Education on cough etiquette and respiratory hygiene for patients and staff.
  • Adequate sputum disposal: individual containers with lid containing 5% phenol.
  • Policies and written SOPs for infection control at facility level.
  • Regular training, monitoring, and evaluation of HCWs.
  • Surveillance: annual TST/IGRA testing of HCWs at risk.

5. Tier 2 — Environmental / Engineering Controls
  • Aim: Dilute, redirect, and disinfect infectious droplet nuclei in the air.
5a. Ventilation Systems
  • Natural ventilation: Open windows, cross-ventilation — simple, low-cost, highly effective in tropical/warm climates (WHO NV Guidelines 2009).
  • Mechanical ventilation: Fan-driven systems to achieve >12 ACH; window exhaust fans for smear-positive TB rooms.
  • Mixed-mode systems: Natural + mechanical; recommended when natural ventilation alone cannot reliably achieve required ACH or directional airflow.
  • Negative pressure isolation rooms (AII rooms):
    • Single-occupancy rooms with <0 Pa relative to corridor.
    • Doors and windows kept closed at all times.
    • Pressure differential verified at least daily.
    • Antechamber (anteroom) ideal for maintaining negative pressure.
    • Air exhausted to outdoors OR passed through HEPA filters before recirculation.
    • Minimum 12 ACH required.
5b. Air Disinfection — UVGI
  • Ultraviolet Germicidal Irradiation (UVGI) — used as a low-cost complement to ventilation, especially at night or in cold seasons when windows are closed.
  • Three modes: (i) direct unshielded room disinfection; (ii) in-duct / room air cleaner; (iii) upper-room air disinfection (most useful clinically).
  • Limitations of UVGI:
    • Misses shadowed surfaces — not a complete surface decontaminant.
    • Overall room effect limited by clean air delivery rate (CADR).
    • Small wall-mounted units give false sense of security; no meaningful risk reduction.
    • Efficacy still being evaluated in LMIC settings.
  • HEPA filtration in AII room exhaust ducts removes infectious particles before air recirculation.

6. Tier 3 — Personal Respiratory Protection
  • Minimises residual risk after administrative and environmental controls are in place.
  • Cannot substitute for Tiers 1 and 2.
6a. Who Wears What?
Person Type of Mask Purpose
HCW in AII room / high-risk procedure N95 respirator (or FFP2 / PAPR) Protects wearer from inhaling infectious aerosol
TB patient (outside AII room) Surgical mask Prevents release of respiratory secretions into environment
Visitor entering AII room N95 respirator Protect visitor from infectious aerosol
Lab staff (culture/DST work) N95 + full PPE Protect from aerosol during high-risk lab manipulation
6b. N95 Respirator — Key Points
  • Filters particles >1 µm with ≥95% efficiency at flow rates up to 50 L/min.
  • Must achieve <10% face-seal leakage — fit-testing mandatory.
  • Models: cup-shaped or duckbill; two elastic bands; malleable nose clip.
  • HCWs unable to use N95 due to poor fit → use PAPR (Powered Air-Purifying Respirator).
  • Limitations in LMIC: cost ($1–$2 per unit), discomfort, becomes flaccid with repeated donning/doffing, no universal fit.
6c. When HCWs Must Wear N95
  • While in the room of a patient with known or suspected infectious TB.
  • During transit accompanying such a patient.
  • During aerosol-generating procedures: bronchoscopy, sputum induction, endotracheal intubation, chest physiotherapy, aerosolised drug administration, abscess irrigation.
7. Infection Control in Special Hospital Settings
7a. Laboratory Infection Control
  • TB bacteriology labs carry higher mycobacterial burden than wards — additional precautions mandatory.
  • Direct smear microscopy: Low-risk if safe practices followed; no personal respiratory protection needed (assuming no centrifugation).
  • Culture / DST labs: High risk; require BSC Class II with 100% ducted exhaust, bio-safe centrifuges with aerosol-seal buckets, autoclave within lab, anteroom entry.
  • Lab located at blind-end of building, physically isolated from common areas.
  • Written SOPs available; lab technologists trained periodically.
  • Shipping of cultures in bio-safe triple-packaged containers.

7b. OPD / Waiting Area
  • Separate dedicated cough station / symptom screening bay at OPD entry.
  • TB suspects seated away from immunocompromised patients (HIV clinic, oncology).
  • Outdoor or cross-ventilated waiting areas preferred.
  • Printed cough hygiene posters in regional languages at visible locations.
  • Separate, well-ventilated sputum collection booths placed outdoors or in a mechanically exhausted room.
  • Sputum collection should never be done in enclosed toilets or poorly ventilated rooms.
7c. Radiology Department
  • TB suspects should be scheduled at end of list or in a dedicated slot to minimise waiting-room exposure.
  • Chest X-ray rooms should have mechanical exhaust to achieve >12 ACH.
  • Radiographic staff wear surgical masks; N95 not routinely required unless performing aerosol-generating procedures.
7d. Bronchoscopy Suite (Highest-Risk Setting)
  • All bronchoscopies on TB suspects must be performed in negative pressure procedure room.
  • Full N95 + face shield + gown + double gloves for all personnel in room.
  • Room left unoccupied for sufficient time after procedure (minimum 99% air clearance = ~45 min at 6 ACH, ~23 min at 12 ACH).
  • Bronchoscopes disinfected with high-level disinfectant (2% glutaraldehyde ≥20 min).
  • Ideally first case of the day is the TB suspect to limit scope turn-around issues.
8. Health Care Worker Surveillance and Protection
8a. Pre-Employment Screening
  • Baseline TST (Tuberculin Skin Test) or IGRA at joining.
  • Chest X-ray if TST/IGRA positive or clinically indicated.
  • Document history of prior TB, BCG vaccination, or known TB contact.
8b. Periodic Surveillance
  • Annual TST/IGRA for HCWs at high risk (TB ward, bronchoscopy, ICU, lab staff).
  • TST conversion (>10 mm increase from baseline) triggers further evaluation.
  • All symptomatic HCWs (cough >2 weeks, haemoptysis, fever, weight loss) — immediately investigated with sputum smear + CBNAAT.
  • HCWs with active TB excluded from work until non-infectious (2 weeks effective treatment + 3 negative smears).
8c. Preventive Therapy (Isoniazid Preventive Therapy — IPT)
  • Offered to HCWs with TST/IGRA conversion after excluding active TB.
  • Standard regimen: Isoniazid 300 mg/day × 6 months (NTEP); or 3-month rifampicin + isoniazid (3RH) regimen.
  • HIV-positive HCWs: IPT strongly recommended regardless of TST status.
  • Baseline LFT prior to IPT in those at risk of hepatotoxicity.

9. Special Considerations — MDR-TB and XDR-TB
⚠ Why MDR-TB Demands Stricter Controls
MDR-TB patients remain infectious for months (treatment is 9–18+ months). Any nosocomial transmission of MDR-TB to immunocompromised patients or HCWs is catastrophic and potentially untreatable.
  • MDR-TB patients must be isolated in dedicated AII rooms — never in general TB wards.
  • MDR-TB patients must never share ward space with HIV-positive patients (NTEP absolute guideline).
  • HCWs managing MDR-TB patients must use N95 respirators at all times within the room.
  • Discharge criteria for MDR-TB patients: three consecutive monthly negative sputum cultures (not just smear negativity).
  • XDR-TB patients: indefinite isolation until culture conversion; strict visitor policy.
  • Facility-level MDR-TB infection control plan must be separately documented and audited.

10. NTEP-Specific Guidelines for Infection Control in India
  • NTEP (formerly RNTCP) released Airborne Infection Control (AIC) Guidelines in 2010, updated in line with WHO 2019 consolidated TB guidelines.
  • Every health facility involved in TB management must designate an Infection Control Officer (ICO).
  • Mandatory preparation of a facility-level infection control plan — reviewed annually.
  • NIKSHAY portal integration: all TB patients notified; helps track referrals across facilities and reduces diagnostic delays.
  • Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana: nutritional support (₹500/month) for all TB patients to support early treatment completion and reduce infectious period.
  • NTEP mandates universal drug susceptibility testing (UDST) at diagnosis using CBNAAT (GeneXpert) or LPA — reduces time to initiation of correct regimen → reduces duration of infectiousness.
  • Use of shorter MDR regimen (BPaL) when applicable — reduces duration of infectiousness from 18 months to 6–9 months.
  • NTEP Circular 2023: All district hospitals & medical college hospitals to have at least one functional AII room per TB unit.

11. Contact Tracing and Outbreak Management
  • All HCWs exposed to an unrecognised source case must be screened.
  • Contact investigation follows a "stone-in-a-pond" approach: start with closest contacts (same room, same ward) → expand to secondary contacts if ongoing transmission detected.
  • Patients who shared a ward / AII room with a confirmed infectious case: TST/IGRA at time of exposure and repeat at 8–10 weeks.
  • Children under 5 years and HIV-positive contacts automatically offered IPT.
  • In case of nosocomial TB outbreak:
    • Notify District TB Officer and hospital infection control committee immediately.
    • Perform facility infection control audit.
    • Source patient genotyped (MIRU-VNTR or WGS) to confirm nosocomial link.
    • All exposed patients and HCWs screened.
    • Temporary ward closure if ongoing transmission suspected.

13. Conclusion
  • Hospital infection control for TB is a multi-layered, system-level responsibility — not merely placing patients in isolation.
  • The three-tier hierarchy (Administrative → Environmental → Personal protection) must function in synergy; each tier compensates for limitations of the previous.
  • In India's resource-constrained settings, administrative and natural ventilation measures offer the greatest feasibility and cost-effectiveness.
  • MDR-TB and HIV–TB co-infection demand the strictest possible segregation and isolation protocols.
  • Protection of health care workers — through surveillance, IPT, and respirator use — is both an ethical obligation and a public health necessity.
  • Effective hospital infection control directly contributes to India's goal of TB elimination by 2025 (NTEP target) and eradication by 2030 (WHO/SDG).
🏅 Examiner's Key Take-Home Points
  • Three tiers: Administrative > Environmental > Personal protection (in order of priority and cost-effectiveness).
  • AII room needs: negative pressure + >12 ACH + single occupancy + HEPA exhaust.
  • N95 filters ≥95% of particles >1 µm; mandatory for HCWs during aerosol-generating procedures.
  • MDR-TB: isolate until 3 consecutive negative cultures; never co-locate with HIV patients.
  • NTEP mandates: ICO at every facility, annual HCW TST, and NIKSHAY notification within 24 hours.
  • IPT regimen: INH 300 mg/day × 6 months for TST converters after active TB exclusion.
