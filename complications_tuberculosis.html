Complications and Sequelae of Tuberculosis 10 Marks

Respiratory Medicine  |  Based on: Sharma & Mohan — Textbook of TB & NTM Diseases  |  India NTEP Guidelines

1. Introduction

  • Despite effective chemotherapy, pulmonary TB leads to a variety of short- and long-term health consequences.
  • Complications arise due to: structural damage, vascular compromise, immune-mediated injury, and drug-related paradoxical reactions.
  • India carries the highest global TB burden (one-fifth of global incidence), making TB complications a major public health concern.
  • Complications are classified as Local (Pulmonary + Pleural) and Systemic.

2. Classification of Complications

🔅 Local Complications

Pulmonary:
  • Haemoptysis
  • Open-Negative Syndrome
  • Aspergilloma (Mycetoma)
  • End-stage lung destruction
  • Scar carcinoma
  • Tubercular laryngitis
  • Tracheobronchial stenosis
  • Right middle lobe syndrome
  • Post-TB bronchiectasis
  • Broncholithiasis
  • COPD / Obstructive airway disease
  • Calcification
Pleural:
  • Dry pleurisy & Pleural effusion
  • Tubercular empyema
  • Bronchopleural fistula
  • Pneumothorax
  • Fibrothorax / Pleural thickening

☠ Systemic Complications

  • Cor pulmonale
  • Chronic respiratory failure (Type I & II)
  • Pulmonary hypertension
  • Secondary amyloidosis
  • ARDS
  • Sepsis (TB-related)
  • Death

3. Haemoptysis

Definition: Expectoration of blood originating from the lower respiratory tract.
Incidence: 30–35% of pulmonary TB patients  |  TB was the cause in 55.7% of haemoptysis cases in a Delhi TB clinic study.

C. Management of Haemoptysis

  • Mild: Bed rest, foot-end elevation, lateral decubitus (affected side down), sedation, IV access.
  • Moderate: Blood grouping & cross-match, transfusion if needed, broad-spectrum antibiotics if secondary infection suspected, ATT for active TB.
  • Massive (>600 mL/24h):
    • Bronchial Artery Embolisation (BAE) — first-line life-saving procedure (success rate 96.4%, Shin et al. 2011).
    • Flexible bronchoscopy — localisation + endobronchial tamponade.
    • Double-lumen intubation to protect contralateral lung.
    • Surgery (lobectomy/pneumonectomy) — last resort.
Mortality from untreated massive haemoptysis: >50%

  • Asymptomatic: Watchful waiting

5. Pleural Complications

A. Tuberculous Pleural Effusion

  • 60–70% of exudative pleural effusions are tubercular in origin in endemic regions.
  • Pathogenesis: Rupture of subpleural caseous focus → TB antigen enters pleural space → delayed hypersensitivity → lymphocytic exudate.
  • Clinical: Usually unilateral; acute onset mimicking bacterial pneumonia; cough (70%), chest pain (75%), fever.

B. Spontaneous Pneumothorax

  • Prevalence: 1–15% in hospitalised TB patients.
  • Causes: Rupture of subpleural caseous focus / superficial cavity / bulla/bleb secondary to fibrosis.
  • More common in males, right side, middle-aged; steroid use increases risk.
  • Management:
    • All patients → Intercostal tube (ICT) drainage + ATT + antibiotics.
    • Mean tube duration: 17–50 days (longer due to extensive destruction).
    • Pleurodesis (betadine/doxycycline) once lung expands.
    • Surgery (lobectomy, pneumonectomy, decortication) if lung fails to re-expand; delay till 6 weeks of ATT.
    • Mortality: ~10%.

Hazards of Bronchopleural Fistula (BPF):
  • Free bacterial access to pleural space → superinfection.
  • Risk of fulminant pneumonia from infected material entering tracheobronchial tree.
  • Chronicity → fibrothorax.
  • Fatal: Massive aspiration into contralateral bronchus may drown the patient.
  • Drug resistance may develop in pleural space MTB.
Management (Jain's 4-Stage Approach):
  1. Closed ICT drainage + ATT
  2. Decortication / Pleuro-pneumonectomy / Pleuro-lobectomy
  3. Saucerisation + Fistula closure + Muscle graft
  4. Thoracoplasty

7. Other Important Complications

A. Post-TB Bronchiectasis

  • Mechanism: Atelectasis → fibrosis → traction bronchiectasis; post-obstructive dilatation from enlarged lymph nodes.
  • Location: Upper lobes predominantly (unlike idiopathic bronchiectasis which affects lower lobes).
  • Features: Recurrent haemoptysis, chronic productive cough, recurrent chest infections.
  • Diagnosis: HRCT chest — gold standard (cylindrical, varicose, or saccular bronchiectasis).
  • Management: Postural drainage, physiotherapy, antibiotics for exacerbations, surgery for localised disease with recurrent haemoptysis.

B. Post-TB COPD / Obstructive Airway Disease

  • TB causes significant and often irreversible airflow obstruction independent of smoking.
  • Prevalence: 20–68% of post-TB patients show obstructive spirometry (Plit et al.).
  • Mechanisms:
    • Bronchostenosis from endobronchial granulomas and fibrosis.
    • Destruction of alveolar walls → emphysema.
    • Recurrent post-TB bronchial infections → airway remodelling.
    • Fibro-cavitary disease → loss of lung volume.
  • Spirometry: FEV₁/FVC <0.70; FEV₁ progressively reduced with severity.
  • NTEP Guidance: All post-TB patients should be assessed for PTLD (Post-TB Lung Disease) at completion of treatment.
  • Management: Bronchodilators (SABA, LABA, LAMA), ICS if reversibility; pulmonary rehabilitation.

C. Scar Carcinoma

  • Adenocarcinoma arising at the periphery of old TB fibrotic scars.
  • Risk: 2–5 times higher than general population for development of lung cancer.
  • Mechanisms: Chronic inflammation → DNA damage → oncogene activation (KRAS, p53 mutations).
  • Clinical clue: New opacity appearing in an old calcified/fibrotic lesion — always suspect scar carcinoma.
  • Investigations: PET-CT, CBNAAT (to exclude re-activation), biopsy with histopathology.
  • Management: Surgical resection / chemoradiotherapy as for primary lung cancer.

E. Pulmonary Calcification

  • Residual lesion — Ghon focus calcification → Ranke complex (Ghon focus + calcified hilar node).
  • Diffuse parenchymal calcification after miliary TB: post-TB miliary calcifications.
  • Eggshell calcification of hilar lymph nodes: classical CXR appearance in TB (also seen in silicosis).
  • Calcification per se is benign; clinical relevance: broncholithiasis, confusion on imaging with malignancy.

F. Secondary (AA) Amyloidosis

  • Complicates chronic/untreated TB, especially fibro-cavitary or bone TB.
  • Serum Amyloid A (SAA) — an acute phase reactant — deposits in kidney, liver, spleen as amyloid fibrils.
  • Presents as: Nephrotic syndrome → chronic renal failure; hepatosplenomegaly.
  • Diagnosis: Congo red stain of tissue biopsy (apple-green birefringence under polarised light).
  • Prognosis: Reversal possible with successful ATT if renal function not yet significantly compromised.

10. Paradoxical Reactions / Immune Reconstitution Inflammatory Syndrome (IRIS)

  • Definition: Temporary worsening of TB lesions despite bacteriologically effective ATT, due to immune-mediated inflammation.
  • Incidence: HIV-negative TB: 2–23%; HIV-positive TB (TB-IRIS): 8–43%.
  • Pathogenesis: ATT → rapid reduction in mycobacterial antigen load → paradoxical overshooting immune response → local inflammation/exudation at TB foci.
  • Manifestations: Enlarging lymph nodes, worsening pulmonary infiltrates, new effusion, CNS TB (raised ICP), new fever.
  • Management:
    • Continue ATT — do NOT interpret as treatment failure.
    • NSAIDS for mild reactions.
    • Prednisolone 1–1.5 mg/kg/day (tapered over 6–12 weeks) for severe or CNS IRIS.
    • Thalidomide for refractory CNS TB paradoxical reactions (limited evidence).

13. Conclusion

  • TB complications span a wide spectrum — from immediately life-threatening (massive haemoptysis, pneumothorax, ARDS, BPF) to chronic and debilitating (cor pulmonale, PTLD, bronchiectasis, amyloidosis).
  • Timely, complete, and supervised ATT (as mandated by NTEP) remains the single most effective intervention to prevent complications.
  • Post-TB follow-up is essential — the concept of PTLD (Post-TB Lung Disease) is now officially recognised by WHO (2020) and NTEP, acknowledging that successful TB cure does not equal full lung restoration.
  • Multi-disciplinary management — pulmonologist, thoracic surgeon, hepatologist, ophthalmologist, cardiologist — is required for optimal outcomes.
  • India's NTEP 2023 goal of TB elimination by 2025 hinges on preventing complications through early diagnosis, universal DST, nutritional support, and systematic post-treatment surveillance.
