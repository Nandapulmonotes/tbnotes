Vaccines in Tuberculosis
10 MARKS
1. Introduction
Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the most fatal infectious diseases globally; an effective vaccine is critical for its
control.
Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG) is the only available and widely used TB vaccine, developed >100 years ago.
Despite widespread use (>3 billion children vaccinated), BCG efficacy is highly variable (0–80%) across populations.
Key challenge: absence of an effective vaccine makes global TB eradication unlikely on chemotherapy alone.
The immune correlate of protection against TB is not fully defined, complicating vaccine development.
Key Fact: Only ~10% of Mtb-infected individuals develop active disease; the remaining 90% control infection effectively
— proof that protective immunity is biologically achievable.
2. BCG Vaccine — History, Composition & Mechanism
A. Historical Background
Developed by Albert Calmette and Camille Guérin, 1908–1921, by serial passaging of Mycobacterium bovis for 13
years (231 passages).
First human use: 1921 (neonates at Charité, Paris).
Included in WHO Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI) in 1974.
Lübeck Disaster (1930): 251 neonates received BCG contaminated with virulent Mtb → 72 deaths. Led to stricter
manufacturing standards.
India: BCG Vaccine Laboratory established in Chennai, 1948; currently manufactures Danish 1331 strain.
B. Composition & Strains
Live, attenuated strain of M. bovis.
Worldwide: Danish 1331, Tokyo 172-1, Russian BCG-I are most used (supplied by UNICEF).
Strains classified as "Early" (closer to Pasteur original) and "Late" (more deletions/mutations) — differ in
immunogenicity.
Dose: 0.05 ml (<1 month); 0.1 ml (>1 month); strictly intradermal route.
Storage: +2°C to +8°C; protected from light; shelf life 24 months.
C. Mechanism of Protection
BCG does not prevent infection but reduces uncontrolled multiplication and hematogenous dissemination.
Generates primarily CD4+ T-cell (Th1) response → IFN-γ and IL-2 production → macrophage activation.
Also stimulates limited CD8+ CTL and γ/δ T-cell responses.
BCG-induced immunity wanes over time; memory T-cell pool is gradually lost.
Low dose → exclusive Th1 response; high dose → mixed Th1/Th2 → reduced DTH, less protection.

Chingleput Trial (Landmark): Conducted in 3,60,000 persons across Tamil Nadu. BCG showed zero protection against
adult pulmonary TB. Hypotheses: high pre-existing environmental mycobacterial exposure, low virulence of local Mtb
strains, and high re-infection rate.
4. Reasons for Variable BCG Efficacy
Host factors: Age, nutritional status, genetic polymorphisms (IFN-γ receptor, HLA-DR, NRAMP, NOD2, Vitamin D
receptor)
BCG strain differences: Genotypic/phenotypic variation across manufacturing labs
Mtb strain variation: Beijing strain — prevalent in Asia, more virulent, associated with outbreaks in BCG-vaccinated
populations
Environmental mycobacteria (NTM): Pre-existing sensitisation may mask or block BCG effect
HIV co-infection: Progressive CD4+ decline impairs BCG-induced immunity; BCG may cause disseminated disease in
HIV+ children
Co-infections: Schistosomiasis, hookworm → downregulate T-cell responses → reduce BCG efficacy
Immune status & re-infection: In high-burden areas, exogenous re-infection may overwhelm BCG-induced immunity
5. BCG Recommendations (India & Global)
Country / Body Recommendation
India (UIP / IAP) Universal vaccination at birth (institutional) or at 6 weeks; catch-up till 5 years
WHO (EPI) Single dose at birth in endemic countries; not recommended in HIV-infected infants (symptomatic)
UK Babies born in high-incidence areas or with family history from high-TB-burden countries
Australia Aboriginal neonates in high-incidence areas; travellers to endemic countries
USA / Canada Not routine; only exceptional circumstances (high-risk infants, MDR-TB exposure)

Re-vaccination not recommended (no added benefit; mycobacterial sensitisation may lower BCG efficacy).
BCG + measles/MMR: minimum 4-week gap between two live vaccines.
Contraindications: Symptomatic HIV/AIDS, primary immunodeficiency, active TB, immunosuppressive therapy.

Figure 1: Overview of new TB vaccine development strategies
A. Recombinant BCG (rBCG)
BCG engineered to express immunodominant Mtb antigens (e.g., ESAT-6, MPB-64, 30–32 kDa protein, 16 kDa HspX).
Lost RD1–RD16 genomic regions (encoding 129 ORFs) can be partially restored for enhanced antigenicity.
Caution: Full RD1 re-introduction → virulence in immunocompromised mice.
VPM1002 (rBCGΔUreC::Hly): Expresses listeriolysin of Listeria monocytogenes → escapes phagolysosome →
enhances MHC class I (CD8+) responses → better cytotoxic T-cell response. Phase IIa
rBCG expressing human Th1 cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12) — improved Th1 but insufficient protection.
B. Live Attenuated Mtb Mutants & Auxotrophs
Rationale: Vaccine antigenically identical to pathogen → better protection.
Auxotrophic mutants (leucine, tryptophan, proline, pantothenate): Cleared from immunocompromised hosts; cannot
replicate in vivo without exogenous amino acids.
panCD mutant: Highly attenuated; safe in SCID & IFN-γ deficient mice; induces long-term memory.
MTBVAC: Live attenuated Mtb with deletion of phoP and fadD26 virulence genes. Phase IIa
Ideal: ≥2 unlinked gene deletions; must survive long enough to generate immunity; balance attenuation vs
immunogenicity.
C. Subunit Vaccines
Comprise secretory (culture filtrate) and non-secretory proteins, lipids, carbohydrates.
Adjuvants critical: DDA, MPL-A, AS01E, IC31, CAF01 → enhance Th1 response.
ESAT-6 alone — no protection; with DDA+MPL-A → protection equivalent to BCG.
M72/AS01E: Fusion of Mtb antigens 32A + 39A; 54% protection against active pulmonary TB in Mtb-infected adults.
Phase IIb
H4:IC31 (Ag85B + TB10.4), H56:IC31 (Ag85B + ESAT-6 + Rv2660c): Currently in Phase IIa trials.
D. DNA Vaccines
Intramuscular injection of mammalian expression vector → continuous antigen expression → potent cellular (MHC
class I) immunity.
Antigens used: Ag85 complex, 65 kDa HSP, ESAT-6, MPT-64, PstS, PPE proteins, HspX (latency antigen).
Fusion DNA vaccines (Ag85B + ESAT-6 or MPT-64) → protection ≥ BCG in mice.
Concerns: Antibiotic resistance markers in plasmid backbone, risk of genomic integration, anti-DNA autoimmunity →
currently no human clinical trials.
E. Epitope-Based Vaccines
Exclusive delivery of immunodominant T-cell epitopes of single/multiple proteins.
Eliminates toxic/immunosuppressive regions; epitopes can be engineered for desired response.
Identified by bioinformatics ("reverse vaccinology") or by T-cell screening using overlapping peptides.
Multi-epitope vaccines incorporating conserved epitopes across pathogens under evaluation; none in clinical trials yet.

Figure 2: Heterologous Prime-Boost strategy in TB vaccination
Homologous boosting (same vaccine repeated) → strong humoral; poor cellular immunity. Repeated BCG — no
benefit.
Heterologous boosting → synergistic cellular immunity.
MVA85A (Modified Vaccinia Ankara expressing Antigen 85A):
BCG prime → MVA85A boost: ~300-fold reduction in lung bacillary load in mice.
Only strategy that significantly prolonged guinea pig survival in EU TB vaccine program.
First new-generation TB vaccine in human trials.
Phase IIb in South African infants: Safe & immunogenic but no protection — highlighted inadequacy of current
immunological correlates.
Ag85A protein boost in BCG-vaccinated aged mice → restored resistance to levels of young animals.
G. Atypical Mycobacterial (NTM) Vaccines
M. microti: 77% protection (comparable to BCG) in prophylactic human trials in infants.
M. vaccae: Killed/live preparation; used in prophylactic and immunotherapeutic mode. Phase III
Mycobacterium indicus pranii (M. w): Reduced bacillary load in mice, prevented granulomas in guinea pigs; 61.5%
reduction in TB incidence in leprosy trial population — but failed Phase III TB trial.
DAR-901 (heat-inactivated whole-cell NTM): Protection against definite TB in HIV+ participants. Phase II in Tanzania
planned.

8. Immunotherapeutic (Post-Exposure) Vaccines
Designed for use after Mtb infection or during active TB treatment to boost host immunity.
Goal: Reduce treatment duration, prevent relapse, and address drug-resistant TB.
A. M. vaccae (SRL-172)
Heat-killed suspension of M. vaccae; intradermal injection.
Proposed: Th2 → Th1 switch; downregulates excessive immunopathology.
Multiple Phase III trials showed no significant benefit when added to standard chemotherapy in HIV-positive and
HIV-negative patients.
Currently, oral inactivated M. vaccae (INMUNE-201) under re-evaluation in Phase III — AnHui Longcom, China.
B. RUTI (Fragmented Mtb)
Fragmented, detoxified whole M. tuberculosis cells in liposomal formulation.
Aims to generate polyantigenic Th1/Th2/Th17 immune response against latent antigens.
Designed as therapeutic booster after 1-month INH preventive therapy.
Phase IIa trial in HIV-negative & HIV-positive latently infected adults — immunogenic & safe. Phase IIa complete
C. Mycobacterium indicus pranii (MIP / M. w)
Heat-killed preparation; used in India for both leprosy and TB.
Demonstrated reduced bacillary load in mice; 61.5% protection in leprosy trial population.
Phase III TB trial in India: Failed — no significant protection against TB in leprosy-endemic population.
Possible role as an immunoadjunct with MDR-TB regimens — under study.
9. Criteria for an Ideal TB Vaccine
Criterion Details
Protective efficacy ≥80% against pulmonary TB in all age groups and all geographic areas
Durability Long-lasting protection (>10 years), ideally lifelong
Broad population coverage Effective in HIV-infected, malnourished, diabetic, and elderly individuals
Safe in immunocompromised No risk of disseminated disease in HIV-positive neonates/adults
Manufacturing feasibility Stable, affordable, compatible with EPI cold chain in LMICs
Compatibility Does not interfere with TST / IGRA diagnostic tests
Protection against latency Prevents reactivation of latent TB infection (LTBI)
Immune correlates Clear, measurable immune surrogate of protection

10. Immunological Correlates of Protection
A key roadblock: no validated biomarker predicts vaccine-induced protection against TB in humans.
IFN-γ production by T cells: Historically used but poor predictor of vaccine efficacy (as shown by MVA85A trial).
Emerging candidates for correlates:
Polyfunctional CD4+ T cells (co-producing IFN-γ + TNF-α + IL-2)
Central memory T cells (T
CM
)
Th17 cells (IL-17) — mucosal protection and neutrophil recruitment
CD8+ T cells and γδ T cells
IgG antibodies against Mtb surface antigens (antibody-mediated protection — emerging concept)
Systems vaccinology markers (transcriptomics, metabolomics)
WHO — established Correlates of Risk (COR) working group to define surrogate endpoints.

12. BCG and HIV Co-infection — Special Considerations
Disseminated BCG disease (BCGosis): Fatal complication in HIV-infected infants given BCG at birth.
WHO (2007): BCG contraindicated in symptomatic HIV-infected infants; give to asymptomatic HIV-exposed infants.
In high-burden countries, benefit-risk ratio favours BCG in HIV-exposed (not confirmed HIV-infected) neonates.
BCG-induced immunity wanes 10x faster in HIV-infected children than HIV-uninfected children.
Novel vaccines for HIV/TB co-infected populations: require replication-deficient vectors or protein-only subunit
approaches (no live organisms).
VPM1002 and MTBVAC are being specifically evaluated for safety in HIV-positive individuals in Phase II.
13. India-Specific Points
India has the highest TB burden globally; accounts for ~27% of world's incident TB cases (WHO 2023).
BCG Policy: Universal at birth; BCG Vaccine Laboratory, Chennai produces Danish 1331 strain for national supply.
Chingleput ICMR trial — landmark demonstration of BCG failure in a high-endemicity setting.
India-developed Mycobacterium indicus pranii (MIP/M.w) vaccine failed Phase III RCT.
VPM1002 — Phase II/III safety trial in India in collaboration with Serum Institute of India (SII).
National TB Elimination Programme (NTEP) 2025 target integrates BCG coverage as a core indicator.
India's ICMR and Tuberculosis Research Centre (TRC), Chennai are global partners in multiple vaccine trials.
