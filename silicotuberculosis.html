Silicotuberculosis

Occupational Lung Disease | Respiratory Medicine

10 Marks Answer

📌1. Definition & Introduction

Silicotuberculosis is defined as the coexistence of silicosis and active tuberculosis in the same patient. Silicosis — an occupational fibrotic lung disease caused by inhalation of respirable free crystalline silicon dioxide (SiO₂) — markedly increases susceptibility to Mycobacterium tuberculosis, making silicotuberculosis one of the most important occupational comorbid lung diseases globally.
  • Reported since the early 20th century; remains a major public health concern in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Prolonged silica exposure predisposes to TB even in the absence of radiological silicosis.
  • TB can complicate simple silicosis and accelerate it to advanced/progressive massive fibrosis (PMF).
  • Covered under India's National Tuberculosis Elimination Programme (NTEP) as a priority comorbid condition.

🏭2. Silicosis — Brief Overview

High-Risk Occupations

Mining / Construction

  • Gold, coal, stone mining
  • Tunnelling, quarrying
  • Sand blasting
  • Well drilling

Industry

  • Ceramic & glass production
  • Slate pencil / quartz grinding
  • Agate industry
  • Iron / non-ferrous metal smelting

Clinical Forms of Silicosis

Characteristic Radiological Features of Silicosis

  • Simple silicosis: Small rounded opacities — symmetrical, upper-zone predominance.
  • Progressive Massive Fibrosis (PMF): Coalescent nodules >1 cm; hilar structures pulled up; peripheral hypertranslucency with bullae.
  • Egg-shell calcification of hilar and mediastinal lymph nodes — pathognomonic of silicosis.
  • Pleural thickening; diffuse interstitial fibrosis.

🌏3. Epidemiology of Silicotuberculosis

  • TB occurs in 20–25% of silicosis patients over their lifetime.
  • Risk of developing pulmonary TB is 2.8–39 times higher in patients with silicosis vs healthy controls.
  • Risk of extra-pulmonary TB (EPTB) is 3.7 times higher.
  • Relative risk of death from TB is 3–6 fold greater in silicosis patients.
  • Hong Kong: 27% incidence of TB over 5 years among 679 silicosis patients.
  • China: 43% TB incidence in silicosis patients over 18-year follow-up.
  • India: 3–7 times higher TB incidence in silicosis patients vs matched controls; slate pencil workers Madhya Pradesh — silicosis in 57%.
  • Increased risk continues lifelong even after cessation of silica exposure.
  • NTM (especially M. kansasii, M. avium-intracellulare) also co-exist in silicosis patients.
India-specific: States with highest burden — Madhya Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, Gujarat, Rajasthan. Unorganised sector workers are most affected with poor surveillance.

Key Mechanisms

Table 2: Mechanisms of Increased TB Susceptibility in Silicosis
MechanismDetails
Macrophage dysfunction Silica impairs phagocytic function; causes macrophage apoptosis; reduces bactericidal activity
NALP3 inflammasome Lysosomal damage activates NALP3 → IL-1β release → inflammatory cascade → fibrosis
Surfactant Protein A Elevated in BAL; allows mycobacteria to enter macrophages without triggering cytotoxicity; inhibits reactive nitrogen species

Nodule encapsulation Bacilli remain encapsulated within silicotic nodules → responsible for TB reactivation

Synergistic effect: TB and silicosis together produce more fibrosis than either disease alone — proliferative fibrous reaction with risk of PMF even in simple silicosis.

🩺5. Clinical Features

Diagnostic challenge: Symptoms of TB (cough, expectoration, wheeze, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue, dyspnoea) overlap significantly with those of silicosis, making clinical detection of concomitant TB difficult.
  • Presentation of TB in silicosis is similar to TB in the general population.
  • Weakness, fatigability, dyspnoea, and night sweats occur in silicosis alone — masking superimposed TB.
  • Haemoptysis, pleural effusion, rapid clinical deterioration — red flags for active TB.
  • NTM co-infection (especially M. kansasii) must be considered, particularly in HIV co-infected patients.
  • HIV + silicosis = profound susceptibility to M. kansasii disease.

Bacteriological Considerations

  • M. tuberculosis bacilli may not be recovered from sputum — silicotic fibrosis prevents discharge of bacilli into airway.
  • Frequent sputum AFB smear examinations recommended.
  • Culture mandatory in areas of high NTM prevalence.
  • Bronchoscopy + BAL + TBLB significantly increase diagnostic yield even when sputum/BAL is negative.
  • CBNAAT (Xpert MTB/RIF) — first-line molecular test per NTEP guidelines.
  • Immunological markers: ↑ Total IgE, IgG, fibronectin, CD4+, CD20+; ↓ mucinic antigen 3EG5 — aid early diagnosis.

Differential Diagnosis

Radiological DD

  • Pulmonary Sarcoidosis
  • Coal worker's pneumoconiosis
  • Miliary TB
  • Pulmonary alveolar proteinosis
  • Talcosis / Welder's pneumoconiosis

Clinical DD

  • Simple silicosis without TB
  • Lung cancer (mass lesion)
  • NTM infection
  • Fungal infections (histoplasmosis)

💊7. Treatment

Key principle: Silicosis per se is incurable. TB is treatable. Management aims at early detection, disability limitation, and treating TB at earliest opportunity.

Anti-TB Treatment

Table 4: Anti-TB Regimen for Silicotuberculosis (NTEP / Standard)
PhaseDrugsDurationRemarks
Intensive phase HRZE (Isoniazid, Rifampicin, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) 2 months Standard NTEP Category I regimen
Continuation phase HRE or HR 7–10 months (total 9–12 months) Prolonged continuation phase due to poor drug penetration into fibrotic tissue
Total duration Minimum 9 months; some guidelines recommend up to 12 months Longer than standard 6-month TB treatment

Why Longer Treatment?

  • Deep fibrotic lung tissue and vascular obstruction (intimal thickening, hyaline/lipoid degeneration) impede drug penetration to target sites.
  • Tuberculous cavities often develop inside silicotic nodules — difficult to reach with chemotherapeutic drugs.
  • Relapse rates are higher — maximum relapses occur within first 2 years; risk continues indefinitely.
  • Success rates of TB treatment in silicosis patients are lower than in non-silicotic patients.

Supportive & Symptomatic Management

  • Remove worker from further silica exposure — essential step.
  • Bronchodilators for symptomatic airflow obstruction.
  • Cough suppressants and mucolytics for symptom relief.
  • Antibiotics for intercurrent chest infections.
  • No role for long-term corticosteroids — may increase risk of TB.
  • Manage complications: pneumothorax, cor pulmonale, respiratory failure.
  • Smoking cessation programmes mandatory in workplace.
  • DOTS under NTEP for all TB patients in India.

Complications

Pulmonary

  • Spontaneous pneumothorax
  • Broncholithiasis
  • Tracheobronchial obstruction
  • Pleural effusion / empyema
  • Haemoptysis
  • Cavitation within PMF nodule

Systemic / Other

  • Cor pulmonale / Right heart failure
  • Type II Respiratory failure
  • MDR / XDR-TB
  • NTM co-infection
  • Renal TB / EPTB
  • Scleroderma (autoimmune)

B. Secondary Prevention — Screening & Surveillance

Table 5: NTEP & Occupational Health Screening Recommendations
InterventionFrequency / Details
Pre-employment medical examination Baseline CXR, PFT, sputum AFB — before joining dusty work
Periodic medical surveillance Annual CXR + sputum examination during employment
Post-employment surveillance CXR every 2–3 years; risk continues after leaving the industry
TB case finding (NTEP) Presumptive TB screening for all silicosis patients; sputum CBNAAT
Notification All silicosis cases to be notified under Silicosis Policy 2013 (India); all TB cases notified under NTEP

C. Tertiary Prevention

  • Prompt treatment of TB with full course of anti-TB drugs.
  • Rehabilitation — pulmonary rehabilitation programme.
  • Disability certification and compensation under Workmen's Compensation Act.
  • Long-term follow-up for relapse detection.

💉9. Preventive Chemotherapy (TB Preventive Therapy — TPT)

NTEP Guideline: TB Preventive Therapy (TPT) is strongly recommended for all silicosis patients irrespective of their LTBI status, as the risk of progression to active TB is very high.
Table 6: TB Preventive Therapy (TPT) Regimens per NTEP Guidelines
RegimenDrugsDurationRemarks
6H Isoniazid (INH) 5 mg/kg/day (max 300 mg/day) 6 months Traditional standard regimen; widely used in India
9H Isoniazid 5 mg/kg/day 9 months Preferred by WHO and NTEP 2022 update for high-risk groups
3HP Isoniazid + Rifapentine (weekly) 3 months Short-course option; better adherence; not yet widely available in India
1HP Isoniazid + Rifapentine (daily) 1 month Shortest option; under evaluation
  • Silicosis patients should receive 6–9 months of INH preventive therapy — reduces TB incidence by 40–60%.
  • INH preventive therapy reduces risk of TB-related mortality in silicosis patients.
  • Active TB must be excluded before starting TPT by sputum AFB, CBNAAT.
  • Liver function tests (LFT) should be monitored — INH is hepatotoxic.
  • Pyridoxine (Vitamin B6) 25 mg/day co-administered with INH to prevent peripheral neuropathy.
  • BCG vaccination — offered to newly employed workers in high-risk industries.

🇮🇳10. India-Specific — NTEP Guidelines & Policy Framework

NTEP Key Recommendations for Silicotuberculosis

  • Universal drug susceptibility testing (UDST) — all TB cases in silicosis patients should undergo DST (Xpert MTB/RIF / Line Probe Assay) at diagnosis.
  • Nikshay portal — mandatory notification of all TB cases including silicotuberculosis.
  • Silicotuberculosis patients enrolled under Nikshay Poshan Yojana — ₹500/month nutritional support during TB treatment.
  • Treatment duration extended to 9–12 months for silicotuberculosis (NTEP Operations Manual 2021).
  • Private sector engagement — all private practitioners to notify and link silicosis-TB patients to NTEP for free drugs and support.
  • State TB officers to coordinate with Labour Department and DGMS (Directorate General of Mines Safety) for integrated surveillance.

National Silicosis Policy (India, 2013) — Key Points

  • Notifiable occupational disease under Factories Act, Mines Act, and ESIC.
  • Employers liable to provide medical surveillance, compensation, and rehabilitation.
  • Silicosis patients entitled to compensation under Workmen's Compensation Act, 1923.
  • Statutory medical examination — pre-employment, periodic, and post-employment.
  • ASHA workers to identify and refer silicosis patients to nearest DOTS centre.
Target: India aims to eliminate TB by 2025 under the National Strategic Plan (NSP) 2017–2025 — silicotuberculosis management is integral to achieving this goal given the large occupational workforce at risk.

📊11. Prognosis

Table 7: Prognostic Factors in Silicotuberculosis
FactorFavourableUnfavourable
Silicosis stage Simple silicosis PMF / Advanced fibrosis
TB type Drug-sensitive TB, sputum smear negative MDR/XDR-TB, smear strongly positive
HIV co-infection HIV negative HIV positive — markedly worse prognosis
Treatment adherence Complete DOT-supervised course Defaulter — high relapse risk
Continued exposure Removed from dusty work Continued silica exposure — progressive fibrosis
Complications Absent Cor pulmonale, respiratory failure, empyema
  • Sputum conversion rates lower and relapse rates higher in silicotuberculosis vs TB alone.
  • Overall 5-year mortality of silicosis with TB > silicosis alone or TB alone.
  • Continued silica exposure despite TB treatment → progression of fibrosis → irreversible loss of lung function.
  • Early diagnosis and prompt treatment with prolonged ATT remain the most important determinants of good outcome.

10-Point High-Yield List

  1. Silicotuberculosis = coexistence of silicosis + active TB.
  2. Risk of TB is 2.8–39 times higher in silicosis patients.
  3. Key mechanism: impaired macrophage function + NALP3 inflammasome activation.
  4. Clinical symptoms overlap — TB diagnosis needs bacteriological confirmation.
  5. Sputum smear may be negative due to fibrosis — bronchoscopy + BAL essential.
  6. CXR hallmark: cavity within or around PMF nodule + egg-shell calcification.
  7. Treatment duration is extended to 9–12 months (NTEP recommendation).
  8. TPT: 6–9 months INH for all silicosis patients after excluding active TB.
  9. Silicosis is incurable — prevention of progression and early TB treatment are key goals.
